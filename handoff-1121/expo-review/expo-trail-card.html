The Expo has a game. It never says so.

The game is 12 stamps, and 8 of them let you go home. Today the player sees a row of dots and a narrator who only jokes, so the tea, the heater and the corridor all read as random. The fix is one plain layer under the jokes: a trail card, a task line, and a stamp that lands on screen.

Second pass, further down: Hoof Sciences and First Press hide their own rules, so both are redesigned and playable here, and every other stop is checked against the code.

    Hoof Sciences, rebuilt as a real captcha

    Checked in the code and its aiming maths, not in a live run. The test does what it was coded to do, and what it was coded to do is a trick nobody is told about.

    • Tick boxes: aiming dead centre at any box presses two of them, never one. Ticks do not stay lit between presses. The round ends after three presses whatever you do.
    • Pictures: aiming dead centre at any picture marks 3 to 5 squares. One square alone only happens when the circle hangs off the edge of the board.
    • All nine pictures are llamas and the prompt asks for squares without one. The only right answer is to select nothing and press Submit.
    • While the narrator is speaking, presses are ignored and nothing shows it.

    Proposed. A captcha everyone already knows how to do, with llamas mixed in among other things. With hooves on it cannot be done, and you can see why. Take the hooves off and it takes three taps. That is the joke, and it is the whole point of the Human Interface Programme. Try it.

    How we steer players

    Six rules to build every stop by. Five come from published developer notes, one from the hoof lab.

    Say the intended action first.
    Then breaking it is a choice the player knows they made. In The Stanley Parable the narrator names the door before you reach the two doors. Our signs do this at First Press and the dome, and nowhere else yet. GDC narrative review
    Stop the player when something must be seen.
    Portal's team added forced pauses, which they called gates, so players would notice the one thing a room teaches. First Press does the opposite today: its big turn happens only when unseen. Portal developer commentary, wiki transcript
    Teach one thing, one step early.
    Portal 2 testers were lost in the last room until an earlier room trained the same action. Hoof Sciences: one big button the hoof can press, then small boxes it cannot, then the captcha. Same commentary
    Pull with sight and sound before words.
    Players rarely look up unless something prompts them, such as a ladder. Schell lists visual design and music among the ways to steer without taking freedom away. For us: wool on the floor, a lamp, a ticking button, a bell. Schell, the lens of indirect control
    Every press gets an answer, and marks stay.
    Mine, from the hoof lab. Ignored input reads as a broken game. If the narrator is talking, the press still clicks and still shows.
    A fresh player every week.
    Portal was play-tested about weekly and changed on what testers did. Someone who has never seen the Expo plays for 15 minutes, and we write down the first moment they ask what to do. Swift and Wolpaw interview
